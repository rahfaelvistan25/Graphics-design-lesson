## Detailed Answer Explanations

### Question 1: Balance
**Correct: A) The distribution of visual weight within a composition**

Balance is about distributing visual weight so the design doesn't feel like it's "tipping over." The lesson compares it to a seesaw — if one side is heavy and the other empty, the composition fails.

---

### Question 2: Symmetrical Balance
**Correct: D) Symmetrical Balance**

The lesson uses a PlayStation game controller as the example — drawing a vertical line down the center shows the left side is a mirror reflection of the right. This is symmetrical (formal, stable, dignified) balance.

---

### Question 3: Unity
**Correct: B) Fragmented and chaotic**

Unity is what makes a design feel like it was created by a single master designer. Without it, the composition feels fragmented, disconnected, or chaotic. The lesson demonstrated this with a SaaS dashboard where all elements share the same visual DNA.

---

### Question 4: Contrast
**Correct: C) Excitement, visual tension, and commands attention**

Contrast happens when strongly opposing visual properties are placed together (light vs. dark, large vs. small). It creates excitement and commands immediate attention. The lesson showed a glowing yellow button on a dark gaming interface.

---

### Question 5: Emphasis
**Correct: A) Emphasis**

Emphasis is the principle that creates a focal point — the area that commands the most attention. The lesson demonstrated this with a cinematic poster where a warrior is isolated under a dramatic spotlight.

---

### Question 6: Repetition
**Correct: D) It builds familiarity and reduces cognitive learning curve**

The lesson states: "Repetition is the glue of user experience." By repeating visual patterns (navigation icons, feed cards), users don't have to relearn how an interface works. Repetition breeds familiarity, and familiarity breeds comfort.

---

### Question 7: Repetition vs. Pattern
**Correct: C) Repetition is reusing an element; Pattern is repetition organized into a formal geometric structure**

This is directly from the Q&A cheat sheet: "Repetition is simply using an element more than once. Pattern is when that repetition is locked into an interlocking or seamless geometric grid across a surface."

---

### Question 8: Rhythm
**Correct: B) Organized movement and keeps the viewer's eye engaged**

Rhythm is described as a visual tempo or beat. Like music, elements are separated by negative space. The lesson compared thin, widely-spaced columns (slow tempo) to thick, closely-packed columns (fast tempo).

---

### Question 9: Movement
**Correct: D) The intentional path the viewer's eye takes through the artwork**

Movement is the deliberate choreographing of the viewer's gaze. The lesson demonstrated this with a three-step visual journey from headline → context card → call-to-action button.

---

### Question 10: Golden Ratio
**Correct: A) A ratio of 1 to 1.618**

The Golden Ratio (1 to 1.618) has been used for thousands of years — from the Parthenon in Greece to the Twitter/X logo and Apple icon grids — because human brains find these natural ratios inherently pleasing.

---

### Question 11: Harmony
**Correct: D) Calm, elegance, and visual agreement**

Harmony is visual agreement — like musical notes blending into a sweet chord. The lesson showed a monochromatic blue dashboard where every color belongs to the same blue family, creating zero visual friction.

---

### Question 12: Monochromatic Palettes
**Correct: B) Because monochromatic schemes reduce cognitive load and signal security**

The lesson's classroom checkpoint revealed: "Monochromatic schemes reduce cognitive load. The mind associates harmonious blue tones with security, competence, and reliability."

---

### Question 13: Variety
**Correct: D) To prevent boredom and monotony while working with Unity**

Variety is the "spice of design." It introduces unexpected shapes, contrasting colors, and different textures to keep the viewer curious. But it works as a partner with Unity to prevent chaos.

---

### Question 14: Conclusion Formula
**Correct: A) Elements + Principles = Effective Design**

The lesson's conclusion formula: **ELEMENTS + PRINCIPLES = EFFECTIVE DESIGN.** Elements are the raw ingredients (line, shape, color); Principles are the recipe (balance, unity, contrast); together they create professional design.

---

### Question 15: Billboard/Landing Page Principles
**Correct: C) Contrast and Emphasis**

From the Q&A cheat sheet: "For a billboard or landing page, Contrast and Emphasis are queen because you need instant attention. For a banking app or reading tool, Unity and Balance take the crown."
