# Slide 1: Introduction: A Century of Graphic Evolution (1920s–2020s)

- **Slide Title:** 100 Years of Design Evolution
- **Core Concept:** The Pendulum of Design (oscillation between Ornamentation and Rationalism) and the 3 driving forces (Technology, Socio-Economics, Generational Rebellion).
- **Screen Visual:** `slide1_intro.svg` — 100-year timeline wave, contrasting poles, and the 3 engine cards.
- **Estimated Time:** 5 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "Good morning, designers! In our first three lessons, we explored the timeless grammar of visual art: the **Principles of Design**, the raw **Elements**, and the architectural **Rules of Composition**.
>
> Today, we step into a time machine. We are going to travel through an entire century—100 years of graphic design history from 1920 to the present day.
>
> Why does this matter to you as a modern designer? Because graphic styles never appear out of nowhere. Design is the visual mirror of humanity.
>
> Look at the screen. You will notice what art historians call the **Pendulum of Design**. Visual culture continuously swings back and forth like a giant clock. One generation creates high ornamentation, emotion, and visual chaos—like 1920s Art Deco or 1960s Psychedelia. And what does the very next generation do? They get exhausted by the clutter, rebel, and demand absolute minimalist order, mathematical grids, and functional simplicity—like 1930s WPA Realism or 1960s Swiss Modernism.
>
> Today, we will study 11 monumental decades. By the end of this hour, whenever you see an album cover, a movie poster, a luxury perfume bottle, or a mobile app, you will immediately recognize the historical DNA that created it. Let's begin in the roaring decade of the 1920s."

### Student Engagement Prompt:
> *"Look at the apps and websites you use right now in the 2020s. Do they feel ultra-clean and polite, or are you starting to see messy, brutalist, high-energy designs returning?"*
- **Discussion Guidance:** Connect student observations to the aesthetic pendulum swinging away from 2010s corporate minimalism.

# Slide 2: 1920s Graphic Design — Art Deco & The Jazz Age

- **Slide Title:** Art Deco & The Jazz Age
- **Core Concept:** The Machine Age, geometric symmetry, stepped ziggurats, sunbursts, and aerodynamic speed.
- **Key Designers:** A.M. Cassandre, Heinz Schulz-Neudamm (*Metropolis*), Tamara de Lempicka.
- **Iconic Fonts:** Bifur, Peignot, Broadway, Futura (1927).
- **Screen Visuals:** `slide2_1920s_art_deco.svg`, `1920s_royal_mail_art_deco.jpg`, `1920s_art_deco_stained_glass.jpg`.
- **Estimated Time:** 6 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "Imagine the world in 1920. World War I has just ended. The Spanish Flu pandemic has faded. For the first time, electricity is illuminating city nights. Airplanes are crossing continents, luxury steamships are crossing the Atlantic, and radio is broadcasting American jazz into European living rooms.
>
> In 1925, the world converged at the *Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes* in Paris, giving birth to **Art Deco**.
>
> Art Deco was the celebration of industrial machine speed, wealth, and modernity. How did artists represent speed on a flat printed poster? Through **geometry**. Stepped ziggurats, radiating sunbursts, and aerodynamic curves.
>
> Look at the work of poster titan **A.M. Cassandre** on the right. Cassandre said: *'Designing a poster means solving a technical and architectural problem.'* Notice how the typography isn't just stamped on top—it is engineered right into the tracks, wheels, and telegraph lines of the locomotive.
>
> Notice the color palette: opulent metallic gold, brass, deep obsidian black, rich ruby red, and emerald green. When you watch *The Great Gatsby* or look at the spire of the Chrysler Building in New York, you are looking at pure 1920s Art Deco."

### Interactive Cue:
Click on the **"Historic Royal Mail Poster"** tab in the media pane to show students Horace Taylor's authentic 1920s maritime poster. Click the image to inspect in fullscreen lightbox.

# Slide 3: 1930s Graphic Design — Depression Era Graphic Design

- **Slide Title:** Depression Era & WPA Graphics
- **Core Concept:** Economic collapse, New Deal federal arts patronage, silkscreen serigraphy with 3–4 flat colors, and Lester Beall's rural communication.
- **Key Programs:** Works Progress Administration (WPA) Federal Art Project (1935–1943), Rural Electrification Administration (REA).
- **Iconic Fonts:** Futura Bold, Gill Sans, Kabel, Condensed Wood Type.
- **Screen Visuals:** `slide3_1930s_depression.svg`, `1930s_wpa_see_america.jpg`, `1930s_wpa_work_pays.jpg`, `1930s_wpa_jessie_james.jpg`.
- **Estimated Time:** 6 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "In October 1929, the party stopped. The New York Stock Exchange crashed, plunging the planet into the Great Depression. Millions lost their jobs, dust storms ravaged the American plains, and decorative gold foil was suddenly replaced by cold economic reality.
>
> But out of this catastrophe came one of the greatest graphic design movements in history: President Franklin D. Roosevelt's **WPA Federal Art Project**.
>
> The U.S. government decided to employ thousands of artists by creating nationwide community poster shops. The posters promoted public health, library literacy, community theater, and national park tourism.
>
> But there was a catch: there was no money for expensive 10-color printing presses. Artists had to use **silkscreen serigraphy** with only 3 or 4 flat opaque colors on inexpensive paper stock.
>
> What happened? That extreme constraint forced artists to eliminate all unnecessary clutter. Look at the famous *See America* poster on the screen. The mountains and geysers are simplified into breathtaking geometric planes.
>
> Meanwhile, **Lester Beall** designed posters for the Rural Electrification Administration. Millions of rural Americans could not read well, so Beall paired photographs with bold red arrows and universal symbols: lightbulbs, water faucets, and radio antennas. Communication became instantaneous."

### Interactive Cue:
Toggle between the **"WPA 'See America' Poster"** and **"WPA 'Work Pays America'"** tabs. Ask students to count the distinct ink colors used (typically only 3 or 4).

# Slide 4: 1940s Graphic Design — Soviet Design, Midcentury Roots & WWII

- **Slide Title:** WWII Propaganda & Soviet Dynamics
- **Core Concept:** Wartime production mobilization, homefront morale, Soviet Constructivist 45-degree diagonal wedges, and female industrial empowerment.
- **Key Works:** J. Howard Miller's *"We Can Do It!"* (1943), El Lissitzky's *"Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge"*, Jean Carlu.
- **Iconic Fonts:** Military Stencil, Franklin Gothic, Futura Bold, Cyrillic Agitprop Grotesques.
- **Screen Visuals:** `slide4_1940s_soviet_wwii.svg`, `1940s_we_can_do_it.jpg`, `1940s_soviet_red_wedge.jpg`.
- **Estimated Time:** 6 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "By 1941, the world was consumed by total global war. Commercial advertising all but ceased. Every billboard, matchbook cover, and magazine page was requisitioned for one purpose: **Wartime Victory**.
>
> Look at the visual language of the 1940s. What is the single most powerful line orientation? The **45-degree diagonal**.
>
> Borrowed from the Soviet Constructivist avant-garde (like El Lissitzky's famous *Red Wedge*), the diagonal slash commands immediate kinetic urgency. It says: *Move! Produce! Conserve! Fight!*
>
> In 1943, artist **J. Howard Miller** was hired by Westinghouse to create posters for their factory floors. Factory morale was lagging, and millions of men were overseas, requiring millions of women to step up to drill presses, welding torches, and assembly lines.
>
> Miller painted a woman in a red polka-dot bandana rolling up her denim sleeve, flexing her bicep beneath a speech bubble: *'We Can Do It!'* Notice the color choices: high-voltage industrial yellow background, primary red and navy blue. That single poster became the immortal international symbol of female strength and industrial patriotism."

### Interactive Cue:
Switch between the **"Authentic 'We Can Do It!'"** tab and the **"El Lissitzky Red Wedge"** tab. Challenge students to spot the shared diagonal geometry in both.

# Slide 5: 1950s Graphic Design — Economic Boom & Modernist Graphic Design

- **Slide Title:** Economic Boom & Mid-Century Modern
- **Core Concept:** Post-war suburban consumer boom, atomic starbursts, boomerang kidney shapes, Saul Bass movie titles, Paul Rand corporate identities, and the birth of Helvetica.
- **Key Figures:** Saul Bass (*Vertigo*, *The Man with the Golden Arm*), Paul Rand (IBM, ABC, UPS), Max Miedinger (Helvetica 1957).
- **Iconic Palette:** Pastel turquoise, coral pink, butter yellow, atomic orange, charcoal.
- **Screen Visuals:** `slide5_midcentury_modern.svg`, `1950s_saul_bass_vertigo.jpg`.
- **Estimated Time:** 6 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "The war ended, soldiers returned home, and the 1950s exploded into a radiant technicolor consumer paradise. Families moved to suburbia, bought televisions, and drove pastel-colored cars with rocket tail-fins.
>
> The aesthetic of the 1950s is **Mid-Century Modern**. Look at the shapes: playful boomerangs, kidney-bean coffee tables, and atomic starbursts reflecting Space Age satellite dreams.
>
> Two giants redefined the craft in this decade: **Paul Rand** and **Saul Bass**.
>
> Paul Rand took European modernism and injected it into corporate boardrooms. He created the logos for IBM, ABC, and UPS. Rand's motto was legendary: *'Don't try to be original, just try to be good.'*
>
> Meanwhile in Hollywood, Saul Bass revolutionized film advertising. Before Bass, movie posters were boring montages of actors' faces. For Alfred Hitchcock's *Vertigo* in 1958, Bass didn't show the actors—he drew a dizzying Lissajous spiral with two black cutout figures tumbling into madness. Bass proved that a poster should be an emotional conceptual metaphor.
>
> And in Switzerland in 1957, Max Miedinger completed **Helvetica**—the font that would conquer corporate communication for the next half-century."

# Slide 6: 1960s Graphic Design — Psychedelic & Swiss Style Graphic Design

- **Slide Title:** Swiss Precision vs. Psychedelic Rock
- **Core Concept:** The Great Polarity: The Swiss International Typographic Style (mathematical grids, objective clarity) contrasted directly with San Francisco Psychedelic Counterculture (melting hand-lettering, optical color vibration).
- **Key Figures:** Josef Müller-Brockmann, Armin Hofmann, Wes Wilson, Victor Moscoso.
- **Screen Visuals:** `slide6_1960s_swiss_psychedelic.svg`, `1960s_swiss_muller_brockmann.jpg`, `1960s_swiss_beethoven.jpg`, `1960s_psychedelic_sun.jpg`.
- **Estimated Time:** 6 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "Look at Slide 6 carefully. This is the ultimate proof of our design pendulum. In the exact same decade—the 1960s—two completely opposite visual styles dominated the world simultaneously.
>
> On the left: **The Swiss International Typographic Style**. Leaders like Josef Müller-Brockmann argued that design should be an objective science. They created mathematical modular grid systems, flush-left un-justified typography, and black-and-white photography. Their goal was zero personal decoration—pure, crystalline communication.
>
> On the right: The Vietnam War protests, the Summer of Love, and the San Francisco rock explosion. Artists like **Wes Wilson** and **Victor Moscoso** designed posters for the Fillmore Auditorium.
>
> Did they care about grids? Absolutely not! They took letterforms and melted them like warm wax until they were almost impossible to read. They paired vibrating complementary colors—electric magenta right against acid lime green. Looking at the poster made your retinas physically vibrate, mimicking a psychedelic hallucinogenic trip.
>
> One style served the corporate boardroom; the other served the counterculture rebellion."

# Slide 7: 1970s Graphic Design — Punk, Moody & Groovy Graphic Design

- **Slide Title:** Punk Rebellion & Groovy Funk
- **Core Concept:** Disillusionment, stagflation, Jamie Reid's DIY Xerox punk ransom-notes, and mainstream groovy Cooper Black earth-toned funk.
- **Key Figures:** Jamie Reid (Sex Pistols), Milton Glaser (*I ❤️ NY* 1977).
- **Iconic Fonts:** Cut-out ransom type, Cooper Black, ITC Benguiat.
- **Screen Visuals:** `slide7_1970s_punk_groovy.svg`, `1970s_punk_fanzines.jpg`, `1970s_fanzines_diy.jpg`.
- **Estimated Time:** 6 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "The optimism of the 60s evaporated into the 1970s: economic stagflation, gas shortages, and political scandal.
>
> Mainstream culture sought emotional refuge in warm, comforting earth tones—avocado green, harvest gold, mustard, and burnt orange. Typography grew bulbous and plump with **Cooper Black** and multi-line rainbow waves on disco and funk vinyl sleeves. In 1977, Milton Glaser drew *'I ❤️ NY'* on a scrap napkin, creating an icon of civic love.
>
> But on the streets of London, bored, broke teenagers had no interest in disco rainbows. They created **Punk Rock**.
>
> Designer **Jamie Reid** forged the look of the Sex Pistols. He didn't have an agency budget. He bought a 5-cent black-and-white copy on a Xerox photocopy machine, cut letters out of daily newspaper headlines like a blackmailer's ransom note, and pinned them together with safety pins.
>
> Punk proved that you don't need expensive equipment or formal design degrees to start an international cultural revolution. Raw, unfiltered passion and a photocopier were enough."

# Slide 8: 1980s Graphic Design — Excess & Neon Graphic Design

- **Slide Title:** Excess, Memphis & Neon Synthwave
- **Core Concept:** Consumer extravagance, Ettore Sottsass's Memphis Group, MTV kinetic motion graphics, neon perspective grids, and the 1984 Apple Macintosh.
- **Key Figures:** Ettore Sottsass (Memphis), Manhattan Design (MTV), April Greiman.
- **Iconic Fonts:** Mistral, Chicago, Neon Script, 8-bit bitmap typography.
- **Screen Visuals:** `slide8_1980s_memphis_neon.svg`, `1980s_memphis_milano.jpg`, `1980s_sottsass_casablanca.jpg`, `1980s_vaporwave_neon.jpg`.
- **Estimated Time:** 6 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "Welcome to the 1980s: Wall Street booms, arcade machines, synthesizer beats, and neon excess.
>
> In 1981, Italian architect **Ettore Sottsass** gathered a collective of young designers in Milan called the **Memphis Group**. They were sick and tired of modernism's serious, boring black-and-grey taste. They declared war on 'good taste' with squiggles, confetti dots, zig-zags, and clashing plastic laminates. Memphis defined the aesthetic of early MTV, *Saved by the Bell*, and 80s arcade rugs.
>
> In 1981, **MTV** launched. Their logo didn't have a fixed color—it morphed constantly into static, zebra stripes, and graffiti, creating the first kinetic motion identity.
>
> And in January 1984, Apple launched the Macintosh. Designers suddenly had bitmapped fonts, digital mice, and pixel canvases on their desks.
>
> The aesthetic: glowing neon perspective grids receding toward a synthwave sunset horizon under chrome typography."

# Slide 9: 1990s Graphic Design — Experimental, Tech-Forward, Minimalist

- **Slide Title:** Grunge, Deconstructivism & Y2K Tech
- **Core Concept:** The desktop software revolution (Photoshop 1.0, 1990), David Carson's *Ray Gun* deconstructivism, and Y2K cyber-optimism.
- **Key Figures:** David Carson (*Ray Gun*), Stefan Sagmeister, Tibor Kalman.
- **Iconic Fonts:** Template Gothic, OCR-A, Distressed Typewriter fonts.
- **Screen Visuals:** `slide9_1990s_grunge_tech.svg`, `1990s_grunge_texture.jpg`.
- **Estimated Time:** 6 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "In 1990, Adobe released **Photoshop 1.0**. Suddenly, designers could apply digital blurs, layer masks, and drop shadows with a single click. In 1991, the World Wide Web went online.
>
> What did designers do with all this new digital power? They trashed the rules!
>
> Surf culture legend **David Carson** became art director of *Ray Gun* magazine. Carson threw out the grid. He sliced fonts in half, overlapped paragraphs until they collided, and pushed margins off the edge of the paper.
>
> Carson famously declared: *'Don't mistake legibility for communication.'* He showed that an article could communicate profound emotional agony or rebellious excitement purely through the way the letters were destroyed on the page.
>
> Meanwhile, as the decade neared the year 2000, the **Y2K Aesthetic** took over: metallic liquid chrome blobs, futuristic 3D wireframe net art, and translucent candy-colored plastics like the 1998 iMac G3."

# Slide 10: 2000s Graphic Design — Simplification & Skeuomorphism

- **Slide Title:** Web 2.0 Gloss, iPod & Skeuomorphism
- **Core Concept:** Broadband, touchscreen interfaces, Aqua glass buttons, digital skeuomorphism (stitched leather, wood, brushed metal), and Apple's iPod silhouette campaign.
- **Key Figures:** Steve Jobs & Jony Ive, TBWA\Chiat\Day, Paula Scher.
- **Iconic Fonts:** Gotham (2000), Proxima Nova (2005), Myriad.
- **Screen Visuals:** `slide10_2000s_web2_skeuomorph.svg`, `2000s_apple_ipod_evolution.svg`.
- **Estimated Time:** 6 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "Entering the 2000s, humanity transitioned from desktop monitors to touchscreen smartphones.
>
> To help billions of people who had never touched a screen before, Apple and web designers relied on **Digital Skeuomorphism**.
>
> What is skeuomorphism? It is designing software to mimic real physical materials. Notes apps had yellow legal pad paper with red margins and stitched leather headers. Buttons were rendered in **Apple Aqua**—glossy, translucent gel capsules with realistic light glares and soft drop shadows that practically begged you to lick them.
>
> Simultaneously, Apple launched the **iPod Silhouette Campaign** by agency TBWA\Chiat\Day. Pure black dancing silhouettes against blazing solid neon pink, lime green, and electric purple backdrops, with glowing pure-white earbuds. It was a masterclass in visual contrast: instant recognition across highway billboards."

# Slide 11: 2010s Graphic Design — Social Media & Flat Design

- **Slide Title:** Flat Design, Material & Social Media
- **Core Concept:** Mobile ubiquity, Instagram square feed aesthetics, the Flat Design overhaul (iOS 7), Google Material Design (2014), and corporate sans-serif 'blanding'.
- **Key Systems:** Google Material Design, Apple iOS 7 Flat Design, Instagram creator carousels.
- **Screen Visuals:** `slide11_2010s_flat_social.svg`, `2010s_material_design_layers.svg`, `2010s_flat_design_vector.svg`.
- **Estimated Time:** 6 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "By 2013, humanity no longer needed training wheels. Everyone knew how to tap a glass screen.
>
> In an instant, the pendulum swung violently away from skeuomorphism. Apple released iOS 7, completely stripping away all gloss, leather, and faux textures. **Flat Design** was born: crisp vector color blocks, thin line icons, and razor-sharp typography.
>
> In 2014, Google unveiled **Material Design**, introducing 'digital paper and ink'—subtle physical drop shadows that indicated elevation and touch feedback.
>
> But the biggest shift was the **Social Media Feed**. Design was no longer printed on large paper—it lived on 5-inch vertical mobile screens. Instagram 1:1 squares, multi-slide educational carousels, and millennial pink gradients dominated the decade.
>
> In the corporate world, historic fashion houses (Burberry, Balmain, Saint Laurent) all abandoned their historic serif crests for identical geometric grotesque fonts to ensure micro-screen legibility on Apple Watches."

# Slide 12: 2020s Graphic Design — Sustainability, Neo-Futurist Graphic Design

- **Slide Title:** Neo-Brutalism, Eco & Neo-Futurism
- **Core Concept:** Post-template rebellion, Neo-Brutalism (heavy 3px black borders, hard 0-blur shadows, acid hues), eco-minimalism (ink reduction, earth tones), and Generative AI frontiers.
- **Key Tools & Movements:** Figma community, Neo-Brutalism (Gumroad), Midjourney / Generative AI, Sustainable Packaging.
- **Screen Visuals:** `slide12_2020s_neobrutalism_future.svg`.
- **Estimated Time:** 6 Minutes

### What to Say (Verbatim Script):
> "Here we are in our current decade: the 2020s.
>
> Look around you. After a full decade of polite, sterile, identical pastel SaaS corporate websites, the pendulum has slammed back once again!
>
> Designers rebelled with **Neo-Brutalism**. Thick 3-pixel stark black borders, zero-blur hard drop shadows, acid neon lime (#ccff00), and quirky sticker badges. It is raw, playful, honest, and proudly un-corporate.
>
> On the physical side, the climate crisis sparked **Eco-Minimalism & Sustainability**: earth-toned packaging, low-ink line graphics that reduce printing chemicals, and dark-mode defaults that conserve digital battery power.
>
> And in digital art, **Generative AI** tools (Midjourney, Stable Diffusion) and iridescent 3D chrome holograms are pushing visual creativity into surrealist dreamscapes.
>
> You are living in a revolutionary design moment. The rules of the past 100 years are your toolkit. Now, let's head to the Knowledge Quiz and test your mastery!"
